Preferences over economic policies are conditioned by the extent to which individuals understand the world in zero-sum terms. Zero-sum thinking (ZST) is the belief that gains for one individual or group necessarily imply corresponding losses for others. We study the prevalence and triggers for ZST among the majority White British group in the UK using a survey experiment administered to 3200 individuals, fielded in the run up to the UK General Election in July 2024. Our experiment presents respondents with qualitative narratives either about the economic success or disadvantage of specific minority groups. We measure how each narrative shifts perceptions towards minorities, and towards the White British ingroup. By examining treatment effects of these narratives on perceptions of the ingroup, our design provides a novel revealed measure of ZST. Estimating conditional average treatment effects, we document that around 20% of respondents are revealed as ZSTs from either narrative. However, each narrative reveals a distinct type of zero-sum thinker: those identified through the narrative of minority success hold similar policy preferences to non-ZSTs. Those identified through the narrative of minority disadvantage are less supportive of the redistributive role of government, pro-poor policies in general, and policies to reduce ethnic inequalities in particular. We show this is not because ZSTs and non-ZSTs differ in how they perceive the economic outcomes, opportunities, or root causes of minority disadvantage. Rather, those identified as ZSTs through the narrative of minority disadvantage, view minorities as contributing less to society overall, and have stronger positive feelings towards their ingroup. Our results illustrate how ZST can be triggered by light-touch narratives typical of transient information exposure in daily life, but consequences for redistributive preferences depend on the nature of narratives individuals are exposed to.
